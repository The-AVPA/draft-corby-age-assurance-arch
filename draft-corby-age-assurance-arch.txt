                       Age Assurance Architecture
                 draft-corby-age-assurance-arch-latest

Abstract

   This document describes a technology-neutral architecture for age
   assurance on the Internet, drawing on the framework established by
   ISO/IEC 27566-1 and the levels of assurance defined in IEEE 2089.1.
   It sets out the methods of age assurance (verification, estimation
   and inference), how their performance is measured and assured, the
   roles and trust relationships involved, and how results can be reused
   across services without creating persistent identifiers.  Its main
   contribution is a model for communicating trustworthy, privacy-
   preserving age assertions between independently governed systems.  It
   treats assurance, privacy, interoperability, resistance to
   circumvention and inclusion as measurable properties of complete age
   assurance systems, and distinguishes the capabilities of existing
   technical components from the additional safeguards necessary for
   secure and privacy-preserving deployment.  It is neutral on whether
   age restrictions should exist, at what ages and for which services,
   which are policy questions outside its scope; it is not neutral about
   how safely and privately such restrictions are implemented where they
   do exist.

1.  Introduction

   Age assurance is increasingly a regulatory requirement.  Services in
   the UK, the EU, Australia, many US states and elsewhere are now
   required to establish the age or age range of users before giving
   access to some content, features or goods.  In most of these regimes
   the regulated service (the relying party) remains responsible for
   achieving the required outcome, even when it relies on a third party
   to perform the check.

   The IAB/W3C Workshop on Age-Based Restrictions on Content Access
   [RFC9998] called for a shared vocabulary, a clear account of the
   roles involved and a neutral mapping of risks against architectures.
   RFC 9998 is an IAB workshop report: it records the views of
   participants and is not an IETF consensus document.  It notes that
   participants pointed to ISO/IEC 27566-1 [ISO-27566-1] as a source of
   common terminology.  This document takes up that call.  It uses the
   framework established by ISO/IEC 27566-1 and the levels of assurance
   in IEEE 2089.1 [IEEE-2089.1] as its conceptual foundation.  Where
   this document groups, classifies or combines those concepts into
   architectural mechanisms, that synthesis is its own and should not be
   read as the normative structure of either standard, so that Internet
   protocols and architectures can be discussed in the same terms
   already used by regulators, certification bodies and the age
   assurance industry.

   The IAB has since published a statement on age-based restrictions and
   online safety [IAB-Statement-2026].  This document shares the
   requirements that statement sets out: disclose no more than a
   minimal, purpose-specific age signal; do not report users' activity;
   do not produce signals that can be linked across sites; do not reveal
   visited sites to the party that verified age; avoid centralised
   stores of sensitive data; and use open, interoperable interfaces.  It
   also agrees that mandates should specify outcomes rather than
   technologies.  It differs in emphasis on two points.  First, it
   treats on-device processing as a property that any provider can
   offer, not only device and operating system vendors, so that device-
   level privacy need not concentrate control in a few platforms.
   Second, it argues that conformity assessment can and should establish
   confidence in claims about system-level properties such as privacy,
   unlinkability and resilience, which the IAB statement notes are often
   missed when vendors are certified against regulatory criteria.

   The goal is to describe how age assurance can be done well where it
   is required.  That means a service can obtain assurance proportionate
   to the risk, users have a choice of accessible and privacy-preserving
   methods, and trustworthy results can be reused without creating
   either a universal identity system or a new means of tracking people
   across the Internet.

1.1.  Related Work

   Several documents address the same problem.  [RFC9998] records the
   IAB/W3C workshop discussion, [IAB-Statement-2026] sets out the IAB's
   position, and [I-D.knodel-age-arch] analyses age gating methods along
   two dimensions, efficacy and privacy cost, organised by trust anchor.
   This document is offered as an alternative architecture to the last.
   It differs mainly in using terminology consistent with ISO/IEC
   27566-1 throughout, in treating assurance as a measurable and
   multidimensional property of implemented systems, in distinguishing
   privacy properties enforced by design from those that rely on policy,
   and in focusing on how age assertions are communicated and trusted
   between independently governed systems.  Appendix A compares the two
   documents on specific points.

1.2.  Terminology

   Terms follow ISO/IEC 27566-1 and IEEE 2089.1 where those documents
   define them.  The definitions below are paraphrased for an Internet
   audience; the referenced standards are authoritative.  Some terms,
   including the forms of binding and triple-blind, are introduced by
   this document and are not defined in either standard.

   Age assurance:  The umbrella term for processes that establish, with
      a stated level of confidence, that a person is of a certain age,
      over or under an age threshold, or within an age range.  It
      includes age verification, age estimation and age inference.

   Age verification:  Age assurance that relies on authoritative
      evidence of date of birth, such as a government-issued document, a
      digital credential or a trusted record, checked for authenticity
      and bound to the person presenting it.

   Age estimation:  Age assurance that derives an estimate of age from
      physical or behavioural characteristics of the person, such as
      facial or voice features, typically using machine learning models.

   Age inference:  Age assurance that draws a conclusion about age from
      other verified facts about the person, such as holding a payment
      card, a mortgage or a long-standing account, or from a combination
      of signals.

   Self-declaration:  A user's unverified statement of age.  It provides
      no independent evidence and is not age assurance in the sense used
      here, though it may be a useful first step in a waterfall of
      methods.

   Age threshold:  The age that a relying party needs to know a user is
      above or below, for example 13, 16 or 18.

   Over NN:  At least NN years of age (for example, a person who has
      reached their 18th birthday is "over 18").  In protocols it should
      be expressed as the inclusive test age >= NN, with the semantics
      defined explicitly.

   Relying party:  The service that uses the result of age assurance to
      decide what access or experience to provide.  It is the "enforcer"
      in the roles of [RFC9998].

   Age assurance provider:  The party that performs age assurance and
      asserts the result.  It may be an independent provider, the
      relying party itself, a credential issuer, a wallet, an operating
      system, app store or another intermediary.  It is the "verifier"
      in [RFC9998].

   Age assertion:  The result of age assurance as conveyed to a relying
      party, for example "over 18", together with information about its
      level of assurance, provenance and validity.

   Level of assurance:  A standardised measure of the confidence that
      can be placed in an age assertion, reflecting accuracy, binding,
      resistance to attack and other factors.  IEEE 2089.1 defines
      levels from Asserted through Basic, Standard and Enhanced to
      Strict.  A level summarises confidence in the result; it does not
      describe every characteristic relevant to a particular relying
      party (Section 3.2).

   Binding:  Measures that link an age result to a person.  This
      document treats three related but different problems separately:
      initial binding (at the original check, the evidence or
      characteristics assessed belong to the person being checked),
      holder binding (the assertion or credential is tied to its
      legitimate holder, typically through a key whose control the
      presenter can prove) and contemporaneous person binding (the
      person presenting at the point of reliance is that legitimate
      holder).  Device possession and successful local authentication
      establish holder binding, not contemporaneous person binding.
      Only contemporaneous person binding directly addresses sharing and
      collusion.

   False acceptance / false rejection:  Accepting a person who is in
      fact below the threshold, or rejecting a person who is in fact
      above it.

   Buffer (or challenge age):  A margin above the legal threshold that
      an age estimate must exceed, set from measured performance so that
      the false acceptance rate meets the requirement.

   Reusable age assurance:  An age assertion established once and
      presented again to the same or another relying party, without
      repeating the original check.

   Interoperability network:  An arrangement under which relying parties
      accept age assertions from providers they do not contract with
      directly, under common rules on assurance and liability.

   Upstream age signal:  An age assertion or age range supplied by an
      operating system, app store, browser, network or device setting,
      rather than by the relying party's own check.

   Triple-blind:  A property of an age assurance flow in which the
      relying party receives no identifying information beyond the
      minimum age-related assertion necessary for its purpose, the
      provider or issuer cannot learn which relying parties the user
      visits, and any operating system or other platform technically
      involved cannot learn which relying parties the user visits
      either.  Relying parties should also be unable to link
      presentations by the same user.  Section 4.7 distinguishes where
      each property is cryptographically enforced and where it rests on
      operational or legal controls.

   Age gating:  Restricting access to content, features or goods on the
      basis of an age assertion.

2.  Methods of Age Assurance

   No single method works for every person or every use case.  Each
   method below can deliver a range of levels of assurance depending on
   how it is implemented, and Section 3 describes how that level is
   measured.  In deciding whether a result is reliable enough for a
   given context, the measured assurance characteristics of an
   implementation matter more than the category of method.  Methods
   still differ in their privacy, inclusion and attack characteristics
   at the same level, and a relying party or user may legitimately weigh
   those separately.

2.1.  Age Verification

   Age verification checks authoritative evidence of date of birth.
   Sources include passports, identity cards and driving licences,
   whether physical or digital; government and other authoritative
   databases; and verifiable digital credentials such as ISO/IEC 18013-5
   mobile driving licences [ISO-18013-5], SD-JWT VC
   [I-D.ietf-oauth-sd-jwt-vc] or W3C Verifiable Credentials issued by a
   state or other trusted issuer.

   To reach a high level of assurance, verification needs two things.
   First, the authenticity of the evidence must be established, for
   example by validating the issuer's signature on a credential or the
   security features of a document.  Second, the evidence must be bound
   to the person presenting it, for example by a liveness-checked
   comparison with the document photo.  Where a credential is held on a
   device, device-bound keys and local authentication show that the
   presenter controls the device, but not necessarily that they are the
   person originally verified: a parent could hand over an unlocked
   device or authorise a presentation on a child's behalf.  Where
   sharing is a significant risk, higher levels of assurance may call
   for contemporaneous person binding, such as a fresh face match
   against the credential's portrait, or for other controls in
   proportion to the risk.

   Verification can also be performed entirely on the user's device.
   For example, an app can read the chip in an electronic passport or
   identity card over NFC, validate the issuing state's signature and
   compare the chip photo with a live selfie, so that personal data
   never leaves the user's hand and only the age result is shared.

   A common assumption is that age verification requires the relying
   party to receive a copy of an identity document.  It does not.  In
   well-designed systems the provider inspects the evidence and returns
   only the age assertion, and selective disclosure credentials can
   prove "over 18" without revealing a date of birth to the relying
   party.  Systems that copy documents to relying parties reflect poor
   practice, not a property of the method.

   Verification is necessary where a specific date of birth must be
   established.  It excludes people who lack accepted documents, so it
   should normally be offered alongside other methods rather than as the
   only route.

2.2.  Age Estimation

   Age estimation infers age from characteristics of the person, most
   commonly a facial image or short video, and sometimes voice or hand
   movement.  It does not establish identity, and a well-designed
   implementation retains no image once the estimate is made.

   Estimation can likewise run entirely on the user's device, so the
   image is never transmitted and only the estimated age or threshold
   result is passed on.  Discord, for example, offers facial age
   estimation processed entirely on the user's device and deleted
   immediately afterwards, while its document checks are sent to a
   provider [Discord-AA].  On-device processing removes the risk of a
   central breach of images, but the result must still be protected
   against tampering on the device, for example by attesting the
   integrity of the software that produced it.  The example illustrates
   data minimisation only; it does not by itself demonstrate resistance
   to presentation, injection or other attacks.

   Older evaluations of facial age estimation, such as NIST IR 7995 from
   2014, do not represent current technology.  NIST IR 8525
   [NIST-IR-8525] and the ongoing NIST age estimation evaluation
   [NIST-FATE-AEV] provide more recent measurements of algorithm
   performance, while the Australian Age Assurance Technology Trial
   [AU-AATT] provides additional evidence from independent evaluation.
   Performance varies between algorithms, age thresholds, demographic
   groups and capture environments, and laboratory results neither
   transfer automatically to a given capture setting nor establish
   resistance to attack.  The appropriate architectural response is
   therefore to set measurable performance requirements, including false
   acceptance rates, and to require testing under conditions relevant to
   the intended deployment.

   The relevant question is not whether an estimate can be wrong.  It is
   whether the error at the relevant threshold is measured and managed.
   Estimation achieves a required false acceptance rate by applying a
   buffer: a user may need to be estimated at 21 or 25 to pass an 18
   check.  Users near the threshold who are rejected can be offered
   another method.  This is the "waterfall" approach described in
   Section 3.5 of [RFC9998], in which lower-friction methods are tried
   first.

   Differential performance across demographic groups is real and must
   be measured, reported and minimised.  It is a reason for independent
   testing and certification (Section 3), not a reason to exclude the
   method.  It is also a reason to ensure alternative methods are always
   available so users are not wrongly excluded.

2.3.  Age Inference

   Age inference draws a conclusion about age from other facts that are
   themselves verified.  Examples include a credit card check with the
   issuing bank, the existence of a mortgage or other adult financial
   product, mobile network operator records showing an account without
   adult content restrictions, the age of an email address and its
   history of use, or a professional licence with a minimum age, such as
   a commercial airline pilot's licence.  Such facts are evidence of age
   only to the extent that eligibility rules guarantee it, the fact is
   authentic and the person presenting it is the person to whom it
   relates.  Holding a credit card shows that an account exists, not
   that the person at the keyboard is its holder.  Some forms therefore
   need more data points, or added binding, than others to reach a given
   level of assurance.

   Inference can be low-friction and privacy-protective when the facts
   are checked by the party that already holds them and only the result
   is returned.  It can also be intrusive if it relies on profiling
   users without their knowledge.  This document addresses inference
   performed as a deliberate, transparent age check, not behavioural
   profiling for other purposes.

   Social media platforms have deployed a form of age inference,
   sometimes called "heuristics", to detect underage users who already
   hold accounts.  Three months after Australia's minimum age law took
   effect, the share of 10 to 15 year olds holding an account on an age-
   restricted platform had fallen only from around 52% to 42%, and the
   eSafety Commissioner concluded that platform implementation remained
   ineffective [eSafety-SMMA-3M].  These findings indicate that the age
   assurance measures deployed by platforms during the initial
   implementation period did not reliably prevent under-16s from
   retaining or creating accounts.  They do not, by themselves,
   establish the accuracy of any particular inference algorithm.

2.4.  Parental and Guardian Involvement

   Some legal requirements concern parental consent or parental controls
   rather than age restriction alone.  These involve establishing that
   an adult is a parent or guardian of the child, and that the adult has
   consented or set a control.  IEEE P2089.3 [IEEE-P2089.3] is
   developing a standard for online parental consent.  Operating system
   and app store settings chosen by parents can be an effective way to
   implement such requirements, provided they meet the conditions in
   Section 5.

2.5.  Reusable Age Assurance

   Once established, an age assertion can be held by the user (for
   example in a wallet or as a device-bound token) or by a provider, and
   presented again later.  Reuse reduces friction and the number of
   times personal data, if required at all, is processed.  It also
   introduces new requirements: the assertion must remain bound to the
   right person, carry its level of assurance and validity and avoid
   acting as a persistent identifier across relying parties.  Section 4
   describes architectures that meet these requirements.

3.  Measuring and Assuring Age Assurance

   The strength of an age check is a measurable property of an
   implementation, not a fixed property of a method.  Two facial
   estimation systems, or two document checks, can differ widely in
   performance.  A relying party therefore needs to understand the
   assurance characteristics of the result it receives that are relevant
   to its context, which a level of assurance summarises but does not
   replace, and needs confidence that those characteristics have been
   independently established.

3.1.  Components of Assurance

   Drawing on ISO/IEC 27566-1 and IEEE 2089.1, this document groups the
   factors that determine confidence in an age assertion as follows.
   The grouping is this document's own; the normative requirements of
   each standard are set out in its published text.

   *  *Accuracy.* For verification, the rate at which forged, altered or
      invalid evidence is accepted.  For estimation, the error in the
      estimate and its distribution, and in particular the false
      acceptance and false rejection rates around the relevant
      threshold.  For inference, the strength of the link between the
      facts checked and age.

   *  *Binding.* How strongly the result is tied to a person, which
      involves three distinct questions (Section 1.2): whether the
      evidence assessed at the initial check belonged to the person
      checked; whether a reused assertion or credential is tied to its
      legitimate holder; and whether that holder is the person actually
      presenting it at the point of reliance.  Live estimation addresses
      the first and third together, because it assesses the person in
      front of the camera at that moment.  For reused results and
      evidence from another source, device possession and local
      authentication establish control of a credential, not that the
      original subject is the present user: a younger sibling who knows
      the PIN, or is handed an unlocked device, will pass them.  Where
      sharing and collusion are significant risks, contemporaneous
      person binding, such as a fresh liveness-checked face match, is
      one control.  Others include short validity periods, limits on
      reuse and risk signals, and the appropriate combination depends on
      the use case.

   *  *Resistance to attack.* Detection of presentation attacks (masks,
      photos or replayed video shown to the camera), evaluated for
      example to ISO/IEC 30107-3 [ISO-30107-3].  Separately, detection
      of injection attacks (synthetic or manipulated media inserted into
      the capture pipeline, bypassing the camera) and assurance of
      capture integrity, for which CEN/TS 18099 [CEN-TS-18099] provides
      an evaluation method.  And controls against collusion and
      credential sharing.

   *  *Bias.* Measured differences in performance across groups defined
      by age, sex, skin tone or other protected characteristics, and
      steps to minimise them.

   *  *Privacy and security.* Data minimisation, retention, purpose
      limitation and the security of any personal data processed.

3.2.  Levels of Assurance

   Standard levels of assurance let a regulator or relying party specify
   the confidence it needs in an age result without prescribing
   technology.  IEEE 2089.1 defines five levels: Asserted, Basic,
   Standard, Enhanced and Strict, with requirements set out in that
   standard.  A level of assurance describes confidence that the age
   result is correct and belongs to the person presenting it.  It does
   not capture everything a relying party or user may care about.
   Privacy properties and inclusion are separate dimensions, and should
   be expressed and assessed separately rather than folded into a single
   level.  Assurance is therefore multidimensional, and a nominal level
   of assurance cannot substitute for understanding the characteristics
   relevant to a particular relying-party context: two results at the
   same level may differ, for example, in how they are bound to the
   person presenting them or in what they reveal.  Certification status
   is not one of these dimensions; it is evidence about whether claimed
   characteristics have been established (Section 3.3).

   This outcome-based approach is converging internationally.  Ofcom's
   "highly effective age assurance" criteria in the UK (technically
   accurate, robust, reliable and fair) [Ofcom-HEAA] and the European
   Commission's guidelines under Article 28 of the Digital Services Act
   [EC-Art28] both set outcomes rather than mandate methods (although
   the Article 28 guidelines prefer verification where access is
   restricted to adults by law).

3.3.  Independent Testing and Certification

   Claims about accuracy, privacy and effectiveness should rest on
   independently verifiable evidence and comparable testing.  Conformity
   assessment, including certification, is a mechanism for establishing
   confidence in claims about a system's characteristics; it is not
   itself one of those characteristics.  Certification provides
   independently established objective evidence that specified
   requirements have been fulfilled, but it does not make an
   implementation effective, and its value depends on what those
   requirements cover.  Three forms of evidence are available:

   1.  *Laboratory evaluation* of algorithms against common datasets,
       such as the NIST age estimation evaluation [NIST-FATE-AEV].  This
       measures algorithm performance, not a deployed service.

   2.  *Conformity assessment and certification* of a whole service
       against the requirements of a certification scheme, such as one
       based on IEEE 2089.1 or ISO/IEC 27566-1, by a certification body
       that is itself accredited by a national accreditation body to
       operate that scheme.  A framework standard does not on its own
       define everything a scheme must test.

   3.  *Test purchasing*, online and face to face, which shows whether
       technology and processes work together in real deployments,
       including against attempts at circumvention.

   The Australian Government's Age Assurance Technology Trial [AU-AATT],
   conducted independently by the Age Check Certification Scheme,
   combined laboratory evaluation and test purchasing with a review of
   each provider's practice statement, in the same way that conformity
   assessment bodies assess solutions.

   Where a relying party performs age assurance in-house or relies on an
   upstream signal, the same kinds of evidence are needed to establish
   confidence in its claimed level of assurance and other
   characteristics.  Assessment should also cover system-level
   properties such as privacy, unlinkability and resilience, a gap
   identified in [IAB-Statement-2026].  How regulators might recognise
   certification is a policy question, discussed in Appendix B.

3.4.  Inclusion

   Because every method fails for some people, inclusion is achieved by
   offering alternatives at the required level of assurance, not by
   lowering the level.  A person without a passport, credit history,
   smartphone or suitable biometric characteristics should be able to
   use another method.  Professional vouching, in which a trusted
   professional attests to the age of someone without documentary
   evidence, is one possible route, subject to independently established
   requirements for its reliability and binding.  No single method
   should therefore be the sole route to compliance.

4.  Reference Architecture

   An age assertion should carry enough information for a relying party
   to trust it, and nothing more.  This section describes the roles, the
   content of an assertion and four reference flows.  It does not define
   a new protocol: each flow can be built from existing work.

4.1.  Roles

   This document uses the roles from Section 3.2 of [RFC9998] and
   describes them in terms consistent with ISO/IEC 27566-1:

   *  *User*: the person whose age is assured.

   *  *Age assurance provider*: performs the check and issues the
      assertion.

   *  *Credential issuer*: a party, which may be a state, that issues
      evidence of age used by a provider or held by the user.

   *  *Holder agent*: software that holds a reusable assertion for the
      user, such as a wallet, browser or operating system component.

   *  *Relying party*: uses the assertion to decide access, and remains
      responsible for compliance.

   *  *Policy selector and rater*: decide which threshold applies to
      which content in which jurisdiction.  These are usually the
      relying party, guided by law.

   party.  Three sets of information should be distinguished: what the
   relying party needs to evaluate the assurance; what an auditor or
   scheme operator needs to establish accountability after the event;
   and what must be transmitted in every presentation.  A rare issuer, a
   precise timestamp or other uncommon metadata can shrink the anonymity
   set and allow correlation even without a user identifier.
   Presentations should therefore carry coarse values where the relying
   party does not need more, for example a scheme or trust-list
   identifier rather than an individual provider, and a validity window
   rather than the time of the check.

   An assertion should not carry the user's identity, date of birth, the
   evidence examined or any identifier that is stable across relying
   parties.  Proposed work on ISO/IEC 27566-4 [ISO-27566-4] on
   interoperability and semantics for age assurance, not yet a published
   standard, would address how independently governed ecosystems can
   express and understand these characteristics.

4.3.  Flow A: Direct Check

   The relying party sends the user to a provider, by redirect or
   embedded component.  The provider performs a check and returns a
   signed assertion to the relying party.  This is the deployment model
   shown in Figure 1 of [RFC9998] and is widely deployed today.  It can
   be made privacy-preserving if the provider deletes personal data once
   the check is complete and returns only the assertion.  Its weakness
   is that the provider learns which relying party the user is visiting,
   and the user may need to repeat the check at each service.

4.4.  Flow B: User-Held Reusable Assertion

   A provider or issuer issues an assertion to a holder agent controlled
   by the user, bound to a key on the user's device.  When a relying
   party requests proof of age, the holder agent presents the assertion,
   disclosing only the threshold result.  Presentation can use OpenID
   for Verifiable Presentations [OpenID4VP], mediated in the browser by
   the W3C Digital Credentials API [W3C-DC-API], with credentials in ISO
   mdoc [ISO-18013-5] or SD-JWT VC [I-D.ietf-oauth-sd-jwt-vc] format.
   These formats and protocols are building blocks.  Selective
   disclosure and unlinkability are different properties: a credential
   that reveals only "over 18" can still carry an issuer signature,
   credential identifier, holder key or other metadata that lets relying
   parties correlate presentations.  Binding to a device key establishes
   holder binding; it does not establish who is using the device at the
   point of presentation (Section 1.2).

   Because the issuer is not involved at presentation, it does not learn
   which relying parties the user visits, provided it does not also
   operate the wallet or receive status or revocation checks that reveal
   them.  To prevent relying parties from correlating presentations,
   issuers can issue batches of single-use credentials (as in the EU age
   verification app blueprint [EU-AV-Blueprint]) or use zero-knowledge
   proofs, so that no signature, key or identifier repeats across
   presentations.  The resulting privacy depends on how issuance,
   metadata and verifier behaviour are constrained, not on the
   credential format alone.

4.5.  Flow C: Interoperability Network

   A relying party asks its own contracted provider for an assertion.
   If the user has already been checked by another provider in the same
   network, the relying party's provider obtains a fresh assertion based
   on that earlier check rather than checking the user again.  Networks
   need rules on equivalent standards, liability and the communication
   of levels of assurance, and must avoid a persistent network-wide
   identifier.

   One design that meets this aim routes reuse through the user's
   browser or holder agent rather than between providers' servers.  The
   relying party's provider asks the user agent for a reusable token.
   The user agent obtains from the originating provider a fresh, blindly
   signed, single-use token stating the age result and level of
   assurance, so the originating provider does not learn which relying
   party or which provider asked for it.  The relying party's provider
   verifies the token against the originating provider's published key,
   obtained from the scheme's trust list.  Because each token is used
   once, neither provider can link presentations.  Variants in which
   providers exchange assertions directly between servers are simpler to
   deploy, but expose the requesting party to the originating provider
   and depend more on scheme rules.

   The euCONSENT project [euCONSENT] piloted interoperable age assurance
   and parental consent between multiple providers across EU member
   states.  It demonstrated that providers can accept each other's
   checks; it did not by itself demonstrate every privacy property
   described here.

4.6.  Flow D: Upstream Age Signal

   An operating system, app store, browser or network supplies an age
   range, usually established at account creation or set by a parent or
   guardian.  The relying party queries it through a platform interface.
   This can be low friction and useful for parental controls.  Where a
   relying party relies on such a signal to meet a legal requirement,
   the signal must meet the same conditions as any other assertion: a
   stated level of assurance, independent assessment and a clearly
   accountable party (Section 5).

   A platform usually establishes the age of the account holder, not of
   the person using the device at a given moment.  Shared devices and
   family accounts make that distinction material.  A relying party
   should treat such a signal accordingly unless the platform binds it
   to the current user, for example through robust multi-user support.

4.7.  Triple-Blind Operation

   A triple-blind flow aims at three properties: the relying party
   receives no identifying information beyond the minimum age-related
   assertion necessary for its purpose; the provider or issuer cannot
   learn which relying parties the user visits; and the operating
   system, browser, app store or wallet provider cannot learn this
   either.  Relying parties should also be unable to link presentations
   by the same user.  Each property can be achieved in two quite
   different ways: by cryptographic or architectural design, so that a
   party is technically unable to learn the information, or by
   operational and legal controls, so that a party that can observe it
   commits not to retain or use it.  The second is weaker and should be
   described as such.

   The last row matters.  Where presentation is mediated by the browser
   or operating system, for example through the W3C Digital Credentials
   API, the user agent can inspect the request and the requesting origin
   by design.  A platform's commitment not to retain that information is
   not equivalent to being unable to observe it.

   Browsers and operating systems necessarily process the addresses of
   sites that users visit, so it is not realistic to require that they
   never know the destination.  The triple-blind requirement concerns
   something narrower: that the platform cannot observe, or does not
   record, that an age assurance transaction took place with a given
   relying party, or its result.  A platform that knows a user visited a
   site, but not that the site asked for proof of age or what was
   proved, meets the requirement.  An age assertion may itself be
   personal data under data protection law, particularly when combined
   with other information the relying party holds, which is why the
   requirement is framed as minimisation rather than as the absence of
   personal data.

   Blind-signature token schemes such as Privacy Pass [RFC9576]
   [RFC9578] can be one component of an unlinkable presentation layer,
   by separating the party that attests age from the party that redeems
   tokens.  They do not themselves establish age, a level of assurance
   or binding to the person, and the Privacy Pass architecture describes
   information that can leak depending on the deployment model.  They
   need a separate age attestation mechanism and protection against
   token transfer.  At least one option that is triple-blind by design,
   not only by policy, should be available in any ecosystem.

4.9.  Trust Model

   Signature verification is necessary but not sufficient.  Before
   acting on an assertion, a relying party needs to establish that:

   1.  the assertion is authentic: its signature verifies against a key
       of the stated issuer or provider;

   2.  the issuer or provider is trusted for this claim, which the
       relying party learns from a trust framework, scheme operator or
       trust list rather than from the assertion itself;

   3.  the level of assurance, and any other assurance characteristics
       relevant to the use case such as binding or privacy properties,
       meet the relying party's requirements;

   4.  the assertion is valid: unexpired, not revoked and intended for
       this relying party; and

   5.  the presentation is bound to the person presenting it to the
       degree the use case requires, recognising that proof of control
       of a credential (holder binding) does not establish that its
       legitimate holder is the person present (contemporaneous person
       binding).

   The trust framework supplies verification keys, trust lists and
   assurance policies to relying parties in advance.  It need not be
   involved in individual transactions, which keeps it from becoming a
   point of observation.  Proposed ISO/IEC 27566-4 work on
   interoperability is intended to let relying parties make these
   judgements across independently governed ecosystems.

5.  Layered Deployment

   No technology is 100% effective, so age assurance works best as one
   layer among several.  Establishing age lets a service apply age-
   appropriate protections.  It does not replace safer design,
   moderation, parental controls or law enforcement.  Service, device
   and network layers can each contribute, and every layer relied on for
   compliance should meet the same standards of assurance and
   accountability.

5.1.  Service Layer

   The relying party is where the decision to grant or restrict access
   is made, and where legal responsibility usually sits.  It determines
   the assurance characteristics appropriate to its risk, including but
   not limited to a level of assurance, chooses one or more providers
   and applies the result.  This document treats the service layer as
   the primary point of decision, informed by assertions that may
   originate at any layer.

5.2.  Operating System and App Store Layer

   Operating system and app store signals can make age assurance easier,
   particularly where parents have set an age range for a child's
   device.  They are well suited to laws that require parental consent
   or controls.  Three conditions apply where they are used to meet
   legal obligations:

   1.  *Equivalent assurance.* The signal carries a stated level of
       assurance, is independently assessed and has an accountable
       party, as for any other source of age assertions.

   2.  *Architectural openness.* The interface between platform and
       service is an open standard.  Relying parties can accept
       assertions from independent providers through the same interface
       (provider independence).  Assertions are portable between trust
       frameworks, and no central intermediary is mandatory, so there is
       no single point of failure.

   3.  *No tracking.* Where the architecture allows, the platform should
       be technically unable to learn which relying parties request the
       signal.  Where it can observe requests, it must not retain or use
       them, and that weaker, policy-based protection should be
       disclosed (Section 4.7).

   [IAB-Statement-2026] considers device-based mechanisms the most
   promising current approach, provided the interface between device and
   service is an open standard and shared devices have robust multi-user
   support.  This document agrees that on-device processing offers
   strong privacy.  It notes that on-device processing is not limited to
   operating system vendors: independent providers can run verification
   and estimation on the user's device (Section 2), delivering the
   privacy benefit without concentrating control in a few platforms.

5.3.  Network Layer

   "Network" is often listed among the layers at which age assurance can
   operate, but there are few examples of it in practice.  Operators
   already apply category-based filtering for subscribers who choose it,
   and in some jurisdictions by default on mobile connections.  Such
   filtering avoids new personal data collection.  The network layer may
   also have a more constructive role than blocking alone.

   A mobile network operator often holds verified information about its
   account holder, including their age, and knows whether the account
   holder or a parent or guardian has set controls on a line.  In
   principle, it could express this as a privacy-preserving age-range
   signal (for example under 13, 13 to 17 or 18+) rather than an
   enforcement decision.  For example, it could indicate to a relying
   party, at the user's request, that a connection is subject to
   under-18 controls, without revealing the subscriber's identity.  Such
   a signal could trigger safer defaults on a service, or a stronger
   check, without the network inspecting content.  Because the person
   using a line may not be the account holder, such a signal describes
   the controls on the line rather than the age of its user.

   Network signals face the same conditions as other upstream signals.
   They must be user-initiated or user-visible, must not create a
   persistent identifier observable by services, and must not be a
   vector for network-level censorship.  They complement, rather than
   replace, checks at the service layer.

5.4.  Geolocation

   Age requirements vary by jurisdiction, so relying parties need some
   assurance of where a user is.  Location assurance should be
   proportionate and privacy-preserving, for example by using coarse
   location rather than continuous tracking.  VPN circumvention is a
   risk to be addressed in proportion to harm.  Any approach to VPNs
   should respect their legitimate privacy and security benefits for
   users of all ages.  Device-based location checks can return binary
   responses that confirm only whether a user is within a regulated
   jurisdiction, not their current location.  Privacy-preserving
   location assurance could also use cryptographic proofs of
   jurisdictional eligibility, provided the underlying location evidence
   is trustworthy.  Such approaches require an explicit trust model and
   measures against location spoofing.  They should avoid unnecessary
   disclosure of precise location or persistent identifiers.

6.  Determining the Appropriate Level

   The appropriate level of assurance for a use case is a decision for
   legislators, regulators and services.  Neither IEEE 2089.1 nor this
   document prescribes levels for particular services.  A level is only
   part of the requirement: the binding, privacy and inclusion
   characteristics a context needs should be specified alongside it.
   The following considerations bear on that decision:

   *  *Severity of harm* the restriction is meant to prevent, and
      whether it is reversible.

   *  *Whether a specific legal age limit applies*, and whether a date
      of birth or only a threshold is needed.

   *  *Incentive and capability to circumvent*, including the likelihood
      of collusion by adults.

   *  *Whether the aim is blocking or tailoring*, since age-appropriate
      design may need only an age band.

   *  *The population affected*, including people who lack documents or
      for whom some methods perform less well.

   *  *Privacy impact* of the methods that could meet a given level, and
      the availability of methods with lower impact.

   *  *The size and nature of an existing user base*, which affects the
      feasibility of checking existing accounts.

   *  *The consequences of error in both directions*: wrongly admitting
      someone below the threshold, and wrongly excluding someone above
      it.

   Core Internet infrastructure (connectivity, routing and DNS) should
   remain neutral, supporting only optional, user-chosen signals.

7.  Recommendations

   The following are this document's technical recommendations.
   Proposals that concern regulation rather than Internet architecture
   are collected in Appendix B.

   1.   *Express assurance in standard terms.* Assertions should carry a
        level of assurance on a standard scale, so that requirements can
        be stated as outcomes rather than technologies.  A level
        summarises confidence in the result; it does not replace the
        characteristics in Recommendation 2.

   2.   *Treat assurance as multidimensional.* Privacy properties,
        binding characteristics and inclusion should be expressed
        alongside the level of assurance, not folded into it.

   3.   *Disclose only what is needed.* Where a relying party needs to
        know only whether a user is above or below a threshold, it
        should not ordinarily receive the user's identity, date of birth
        or the evidence used.

   4.   *Do not retain or repurpose.* Personal data used for age
        assurance should not be kept beyond what is needed to provide,
        improve and audit the service or meet legal obligations, with
        transparency about any retention.

   5.   *Offer at least one option that is triple-blind by design.*
        Every ecosystem should include a route in which neither the
        relying party, the provider nor the platform can build a record
        of the age assurance transactions a user makes, enforced
        technically rather than only by policy.

   6.   *Make assurance information available along the chain.* Level of
        assurance, provenance and validity should remain determinable
        when an assertion is reused or passed between systems, through
        the presentation or the trust framework, without exposing more
        metadata than the relying party needs.

   7.   *Reuse without tracking.* Interoperability must not depend on a
        persistent identifier that allows relying parties or providers
        to correlate a user's activity.

   8.   *Build in architectural openness.* Open interfaces, provider
        independence, portability of assertions between trust frameworks
        and no mandatory central intermediary.

   9.   *Offer alternatives.* No single method should be the only route.
        Users without documents, credit histories, smartphones or
        suitable biometric characteristics need another way through.

   10.  *Treat age as distinct from identity.* Age can be derived from
        identity, but it can also be established without identity, and
        some users will prefer that.

   11.  *Avoid unnecessary checks.* Relying parties should not request
        age assertions for content or functions with no age-related
        requirement, and should restrict only the content or features an
        obligation covers rather than a whole service.

   12.  *Handle failure explicitly.* Architectures should define
        fallback methods, routes to challenge and correct a wrong
        determination, and behaviour when a provider or status service
        is unavailable (Section 8.1).

   13.  *Standardise the missing pieces.* The IETF, W3C, OpenID
        Foundation, ISO and IEEE should coordinate on how a website
        expresses an age requirement, how assurance metadata is carried
        and how unlinkable reuse is achieved, building on existing work
        rather than a separate age assurance stack.

8.  Security Considerations

   No age assurance system is immune from attack.  Mitigations should be
   proportionate to the risk of the use case and tested independently.

   The architecture should be assessed against at least the following
   adversaries: a user below the threshold seeking access; an adult
   colluding with that user, for example by completing a check or
   lending a credential or device; a compromised or malicious wallet or
   holder agent; a dishonest or compromised issuer or provider; a
   malicious relying party seeking to identify or track users; and
   compromised ecosystem infrastructure, such as a trust list or status
   service.  Privacy and assurance claims should state which of these
   adversaries they address.

   *  *Presentation attacks.* Masks, printed photos and replayed video
      presented to a camera.  Mitigated by presentation attack detection
      evaluated to ISO/IEC 30107-3.

   *  *Injection attacks.* Synthetic or manipulated media injected into
      the capture pipeline, bypassing the camera.  Mitigated by capture
      integrity checks and injection attack detection, evaluated for
      example to CEN/TS 18099.

   *  *Forged or altered evidence.* Mitigated by validating issuer
      signatures on digital credentials and security features on
      documents.

   *  *Credential sharing and collusion.* An adult passing a reusable
      assertion, or performing a check, on behalf of a minor.  Device
      possession and local authentication do not prevent this on their
      own: a parent can hand over an unlocked device or its PIN, and a
      local biometric unlock shows only that an enrolled user is
      present.  Contemporaneous person binding, short validity periods
      and limits on additional enrolment raise the bar for higher-risk
      uses.

   *  *Replay.* Reuse of an assertion at another relying party or
      another time.  Mitigated by audience restriction, nonces and
      expiry.

   *  *Malicious relying parties.* Collecting excess metadata or linking
      presentations.  Mitigated by minimal, coarse metadata and single-
      use or zero-knowledge presentations.

   *  *Compromised trust infrastructure.* A subverted trust list or
      status service could admit bad issuers or track status checks.
      Mitigated by distributing trust lists in advance, privacy-
      preserving status mechanisms and audit of scheme operators.

   *  *Phishing and normalisation of data requests.* Users who become
      used to sharing documents with unfamiliar sites are more exposed
      to fraud, as noted in [RFC9998].  Mitigated by architectures that
      never send documents to relying parties, by recognisable and
      certified providers, and by user-held credentials presented
      through trusted browser or wallet interfaces.

   *  *Breach of provider data.* Mitigated by not retaining personal
      data after the check, by on-device processing, by separating
      providers from relying parties and by security controls whose
      effectiveness is independently assessed.  Reported breaches of
      retained identity documents illustrate why minimisation and non-
      retention matter.

   *  *Concentration risk.* Reliance on a single platform or provider
      creates a single point of failure and attack.  A plural,
      interoperable market reduces it.

8.1.  Failure Handling

   Every method fails for some people, and every service is sometimes
   unavailable.  Architectures should define:

   *  what happens when a method cannot reach a result, normally by
      offering another method at the same level rather than a lower one;

   *  how a person can challenge and correct a wrong determination,
      including one made by estimation or inference, without disclosing
      more data than the original check required;

   *  what a relying party does when a provider, issuer or status
      service is unavailable, and whether it fails open or closed given
      the risk; and

   *  how revocation of a compromised issuer, key or credential reaches
      relying parties.

9.  Privacy Considerations

   Privacy should be designed into age assurance rather than traded
   against effectiveness.  The architectures in Section 4 show that
   independently measurable effectiveness, strong privacy protections
   and interoperability can coexist, provided the full system is
   engineered and assessed to deliver them.

   *  *Minimisation.* Relying parties receive a threshold result, not
      identity, date of birth or evidence.  Providers process only what
      the chosen method needs.

   *  *Retention.* Images, documents and other inputs are deleted once
      the check is complete, except where retention is needed to audit
      the service or meet a legal obligation, and that retention is
      disclosed.

   *  *Purpose limitation.* Data processed for age assurance is not
      reused for profiling, advertising or other purposes.

   *  *Unlinkability.* Neither providers, issuers nor platforms should
      be able to learn which relying parties a user visits, and relying
      parties should not be able to correlate one user across services.
      Batch-issued single-use credentials, zero-knowledge proofs and
      blind-signature tokens support this.

   *  *Organisational separation.* Independent providers keep the
      personal data used in a check separate from the relying party,
      which may have commercial incentives to collect it.  Independent
      providers are also more easily regulated and audited.

   *  *Age is not identity.* Methods that establish age without
      identity, such as estimation, or on-device age verification give
      users a choice not to disclose identity at all.

   *  *Location.* Jurisdiction can often be established at a coarse
      level, avoiding precise or continuous geolocation and disclosing
      only whether or not a user is within a regulated jurisdiction for
      a particular use case.

   Some privacy costs remain even in well-designed systems, for example
   where a platform mediates presentation or a provider must briefly
   process a face or document.  The properties above reduce them
   substantially, and can be specified in standards, tested in
   certification and enforced by data protection regulators.

10.  Scope and Policy Neutrality

   This document does not address whether age restrictions should be
   imposed, on which content or services, or at what ages.  Those are
   decisions for legislatures, regulators and services, and they engage
   rights that must be balanced, including children's rights under the
   UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and the rights to privacy
   and freedom of expression of users of all ages.

   This document is neutral on whether restrictions should exist, but it
   is not neutral about how safely and privately they should be
   implemented where they do exist.  An architecture that implements a
   restriction should protect freedom of expression, access to
   information and association by minimising what is disclosed and by
   avoiding checks where no requirement applies.  It should also provide
   a route to challenge and correct a wrong determination, since
   children and adults whose age is incorrectly assessed may otherwise
   lose access to lawful content or services.

   Whether a jurisdiction should impose an age restriction, what age
   should apply and to which services are not questions the IETF needs
   to resolve in order to produce useful Internet architecture, and this
   document expresses no view on them.  The same applies to broader
   policy arguments, such as whether age assurance burdens all users
   disproportionately.  Technical feasibility, by contrast, is within
   scope: verification, estimation and inference are deployed at scale
   today under regimes in the UK, the EU, Australia and US states.
   Deployment shows feasibility; it does not by itself demonstrate
   effectiveness, privacy or inclusive access, which must be measured
   (Section 3).  The technical community can contribute most by making
   whatever restrictions are adopted as accurate, private, inclusive and
   accountable as possible.

Appendix B.  Policy Considerations

   This appendix collects proposals that concern regulation rather than
   Internet architecture.  They are offered for context and are not
   requirements of this document.

   *  *Outcome-based mandates.* Requirements should specify the
      assurance outcomes needed, such as a level of assurance together
      with any binding or privacy characteristics the context requires,
      rather than particular technologies, consistent with
      [IAB-Statement-2026], [Ofcom-HEAA] and [EC-Art28].

   *  *Certification.* Providers, in-house checks and upstream signals
      relied on for compliance could be certified under schemes that
      cover system-level properties as well as accuracy, supported by
      test purchasing.

   *  *Presumption of compliance.* Regulators could consider a
      presumption of compliance for relying parties that use a certified
      provider at the required level.  This is not current law in most
      jurisdictions: Ofcom, for example, does not accredit or approve
      individual providers, and a service remains responsible for its
      own compliance.

   *  *No mandated sole route.* State credentials, wallets and platform
      signals are valuable sources of age assurance, but should not be
      mandated as the sole or privileged route for private sector uses.
      Independent providers should be able to consume them.

   *  *Enforcement.* Consistent enforcement, including against offshore
      non-compliant services, avoids penalising compliant services and
      displacing users to riskier ones.
